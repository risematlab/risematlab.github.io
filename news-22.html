Phase-change materials and concrete

Can concrete store heat without giving up strength?

That’s the central question behind our review in the Journal of Materials in Civil Engineering, led by Dr. Xiaoqiang Ni, a recent PhD graduate of our RISE Materials Lab, and now a Structural Engineer III at WSP.

Phase change materials (PCMs) can turn concrete into a thermal battery — absorbing and releasing heat as they melt and solidify. The catch: every incorporation method trades away some mechanical performance or durability. This review maps where those trade-offs are worth it.

Key takeaways:
1️⃣ Incorporation method matters more than PCM type: direct mixing, microencapsulation, and porous carriers each shift the strength–thermal balance differently.
2️⃣ In pavements, PCMs can reduce temperature differences within slabs, helping limit curling and thermal cracking.
3️⃣ Numerical modeling can be the design tool: it lets engineers optimize PCM dosage and placement before casting a single specimen.
4️⃣ Limited field data leave questions about long-term performance, including leakage and stability through repeated heating and cooling cycles.

The goal of this paper is to give practitioners a design roadmap (not just a literature summary) for moving PCM concrete from the lab to real infrastructure, where our lab has made progress by installing self-heating self-healing bendable concrete on MSU campus.

Congratulations to Dr. Ni, and thanks to our co-authors, Dr. Wenyu Liao, Dr. Hongyan Ma, and Álvaro Paul for the collaboration.
